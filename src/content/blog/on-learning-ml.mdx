I watched a lot of ML roadmaps. YouTube playlists, Reddit arguments about Andrew Ng vs fast.ai, fancy Notion templates. Three different ones. Each one lost me at the same spot: understanding something in theory doesn't mean you can build with it.
That gap between knowing and doing is brutal.

### What Actually Worked

I stopped following roadmaps and started with a problem I could see every semester: course selection at UTP is a guess. You ask friends, check the lecturer's reputation, wonder if you can handle the workload. Nobody really knows until it's Week 3.
Building a predictor forced me to learn what mattered. Feature engineering to make sense of course data. Classification to predict difficulty levels. Model validation to know if the thing actually works. Not because a roadmap said so, but because I needed it.
The messy projects are the ones that stick. You hit a wall, spend three hours reading Stack Overflow, finally get it working at 11pm. You remember those.

### The Uncomfortable Part

Your knowledge will be lopsided. After this project you'll understand classification and feature engineering. Probably nothing about time series or neural networks. That used to bother me. But the goal was never to know everything. It was to build something people actually use.

### The Path Forward
Weeks 1-2: Collect and explore course data. What do we even have? Lecturer names, enrollment sizes, past GPA requirements, student reviews on private channels. Spend time just looking at it. Make some plots. Get comfortable with the data.

Weeks 3-4: Clean the data and build features. How do you turn "Dr. Ahmed is tough" into numbers? What if a course has 50 students vs 150? Does time of day matter (8am lectures harder to pass)? Start combining raw numbers into things the model can actually use.

Weeks 5-6: Train a few models. Logistic regression as the baseline. Random forest next. See what works. Most of this is running code and watching the accuracy numbers move. When they don't move, that's when you dig.

Week 7: Figure out what actually matters. Random forest can tell you which features mattered most. Turns out enrollment size might be noise. Lecturer probably isn't. Use that to refine.

Week 8-9: Build the interface. Streamlit is fast for this. Drop a course selector, hit a button, see the prediction. Takes maybe 50 lines of code. Suddenly it's not just a jupyter notebook. It's a tool.

Week 10: Put it on GitHub. Deploy it somewhere free so you can send the link. That's the difference between "I trained a model" and "Here's the thing I built."

That's 10 weeks at 1-2 hours a week. You end up with a portfolio piece, a GitHub repo, and an actual tool people use. Better than any roadmap told me to do.
